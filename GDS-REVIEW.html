03

The pattern behind most of the corrections

The errors are not scattered. They sit in one place, and it is a place you can watch for.

Exact figures are exact; approximate figures are wrong

Every table where you wrote precise numbers verifies. Every table where you wrote a "~" range is wrong in at least one cell. Three tables use tilde ranges, and all three are wrong — in the worst case the range you published contains none of your hundred measurements.

Why this happens, and the habit that stops it

The tilde is doing something specific: it marks the moment you needed a number, didn't have it in front of you, and wrote one that looked about right rather than re-opening a finished script. It reads as a rounding, so it doesn't feel like a guess — but a rounding stays near the truth and these don't.

The habit worth building: a tilde in a draft is a to-do, not a value. If a number isn't computed yet, leave it as TODO so the checker catches it — that's what the placeholder list is for.

The pack states this as its rule and it is worth re-reading. "Never guess in a deliverable. 'I could not determine this; here is what I checked and why it was inconclusive' is a complete, useful answer. A confident wrong number is not, because somebody will build on it." Nothing here is a telling-off — these are much better caught now than after somebody has built on them.

The crime bridge finding needs its base rate

One synthesis claim measures a property of the graph rather than of the algorithm.

Synthesis claim #13, marked High confidence "Similarity misses bridges: 100% zero Jaccard on crime top-1% edges."
What the 100% is actually measuring

crime is bipartite — every edge joins a person to a crime, never a person to a person. Two nodes that are directly connected sit on opposite sides, so they cannot share a neighbour, so their Jaccard similarity is zero by construction. The 100% isn't telling us similarity misses bridges. It's telling us the graph is bipartite.

The comparison that was missing

similarity_vs_bridges.csv holds all 1,476 crime edges. All 1,476 have Jaccard = 0, not just the 14 in the top 1% — so the top 1% is not distinguishable from any other slice. You had already identified crime as bipartite by name in Part 2A §3.1 and used it as the bipartite exemplar in Part 4, so the fact was in hand.

The real finding is the one sitting underneath it

On email-Eu-core, 70% of top-1% bridge edges have zero similarity against a base rate of 1.8% — a 39× enrichment. That's a strong, genuine result about similarity being blind to bridges, and it's currently being outshouted by a 100% that means nothing.

One of these is our fault, not yours

The Part 1 checker passes a Part 1 memo that is one hundred percent unwritten template.

What this means for the feedback above

You ran check_part1.py, it printed PASS, and you moved on. That was a reasonable thing to do — the checker's own docstring promises it verifies "your memo has its sections and no leftover placeholders", and against the templates we ship it can do neither. Part of §09 is a defect in the pack rather than something you missed.

Two independent holes, both in gdslab/check.py

1. The placeholder list doesn't match our own placeholders. check.py:24 reads PLACEHOLDERS = ("TODO", "TBD", "FIXME", "XXX", "<fill in>", "lorem ipsum"). The templates ship <your name>, <the question you were answering> and <start> to <end>. None of them match, so require_no_placeholders is blind to the exact strings we put in the files.

2. The sample-size check is satisfied by the template's own instructions. require_a_sample_size looks for patterns like \b\d+\s+runs?\b and sample size. The blank template already contains the illustrative sentence "Across 50 runs on 5 graphs, 3.2% of communities were internally disconnected" and the heading "Sample sizes and caveats" — so the template satisfies the check just by existing.

require_sections passes by construction, since a template is defined by having the headings. first-impressions.md and research-log.md are tested for existence only, never for content.

The checker isn't broken in general — this was tested before being believed

Two deliberate failure cases were run against it first. Appending TODO produces "still contains placeholder text: ['TODO']"; removing one heading produces "missing required section(s): ['What surprised me']". The machinery works — it just can't see our own templates.

This is the habit §01 mentions, applied to someone else's tool: before trusting a green light, give the thing a case it ought to fail, and check that it does. A checker that has never been seen to fail isn't evidence of anything yet.

This is exactly the kind of thing the pack asks for. START_HERE.md §5: "If you think something here is wrong… that is a finding, and we want it. Finding errors in this pack is genuinely welcome; several exist that we do not know about yet." This was one of them. If you hit others in the correction pass, they belong in questions.md under "Things in this pack I think are wrong".
