Project Summary

The GDS Measurement Program is a rigorous, reproducible benchmark of graph algorithms on 7 real-world networks, built by PrajnaAI as part of the intern-gds project. Every number traces to a CSV row. Every result has provenance.

What we did: We profiled 7 structurally diverse graphs, measured PageRank convergence and timing across 10 seeds per graph, tested four centrality measures for agreement, ran Louvain 200 times per graph for stability, benchmarked betweenness approximation against exact computation at 5 sampling fractions, compared Louvain vs Leiden community quality, and documented the consequences of bipartite projection choices.

What we found: Degree skew explains PageRank iterations better than node count. Louvain is stable on crime (mod 0.9296 ± 0.0004) but noisy on openflights. Projection is NOT neutral — crime side 1 Spearman=0.002 (uncorrelated). Betweenness approximation achieves 95–104x speedup with Spearman >0.92 at 1% pivot sampling. At 5% sampling, speedup is ~18–20× with Spearman >0.97 — the practical sweet spot.

Current status: Phases 0–4 complete and all checkers pass. Phase 5 is in progress: C01 has been verified and rejected, while C02–C12 remain pending primary-source research. The project is production-ready for the measurement phases; the claims strand is not yet fully closed.

What is Graph Data Science (GDS)?

Graph Data Science applies algorithms, statistical methods, and ML techniques to graph-structured data. Unlike tabular data, graphs represent relationships explicitly: nodes (entities) connected by edges (relationships).

Graphs are everywhere: social networks, supply chains, biological pathways, citation networks, transportation systems, knowledge graphs. The structure of a graph — its density, skew, component count, weight semantics — fundamentally determines which algorithms work and what results mean.

GDS differs from traditional ML in one crucial way: the features are the graph itself. You don't extract features from nodes — you compute them from topology. PageRank measures influence by following links. Betweenness measures bridge importance by counting shortest paths. Community detection groups nodes by connection patterns. Each algorithm answers a different question about the same structure.

🔗 Relationship-First

Most real-world problems are about connections — social networks, supply chains, biological pathways, citation networks. All are fundamentally graph-shaped.

🧮 Algorithmic Richness

Centrality (who is important?), community detection (who belongs together?), similarity (who is similar?), prediction (what connections will form?).

⚙️ Production Tooling

Neo4j GDS, NetworkX, igraph, graph-tool — production-grade implementations of graph algorithms.

🔗 Interoperability

Graph algorithms combine with traditional ML pipelines. Results export as linked data (RDF/OWL) for maximum interoperability.

🎯 This Project: We built a reproducible measurement program that benchmarks graph algorithms on 7 real-world networks, validates approximations against exact computation, and exports results as linked data using the Biolink Model vocabulary.

Project Aims & Objectives

Primary Aim: Build a rigorous, reproducible, and auditable measurement program for graph algorithms on real-world networks, with full provenance tracking and linked-data export.

This project is not about finding the "best" algorithm — it is about measuring what each algorithm does, under what conditions, and with what confidence. Every claim must be traceable to a primary source or to our own measurement. Every result must have provenance.

The measurement philosophy is simple: run algorithms multiple times with different seeds, measure both correctness and cost, compare against exact baselines where possible, and document every decision. If we cannot measure it, we cannot claim it.

Six Objectives

1️⃣ Measurement Rigour

Profile 7 structurally diverse graphs. Measure algorithm performance with proper timing, multiple seeds, cross-machine calibration. Every number traceable to a CSV row.

2️⃣ Claims Verification

Trace 12 specific claims about graph algorithms to primary sources. Grade evidence quality. Record verdicts in structured JSONL format.

3️⃣ Approximation Validation

Answer: "Can we sample?" — specifically for betweenness centrality. Determine minimum pivot fraction preserving ranking fidelity while achieving speedup.

4️⃣ Projection Impact

Document consequences of bipartite projection choices. Show projection is not a neutral transformation — can fundamentally change ranking and community structure.

5️⃣ Ontology & Interop

Model experimental results as RDF/OWL linked data using Biolink Model vocabulary. Validate with SHACL. Enable SPARQL queryability for cross-lab interoperability.

6️⃣ Reproducibility

Every result reproducible by another researcher with same environment specs. Runner scripts and provenance sidecars for all outputs.

✅ Validation Pipeline

graph LR A(("Results")) --> B(("gds-owl")) B --> C(("Turtle")) C --> D(("SHACL")) D --> E{"Conform?"} E -->|Yes| F(("Archive")) E -->|No| G(("Fix")) G --> C
Key Advantage: W3C standards (Turtle and RDF) have decades-long archival stability compared to CSV/Markdown. Results can be joined with external knowledge graphs via SPARQL without schema mapping.
📌 Why RDF/OWL: CSV files are opaque — you need to know the schema to understand what each column means. RDF Turtle is self-describing: every triple has a subject, predicate, and object, all of which are URIs that resolve to definitions. This means any RDF-aware tool can query the results without prior knowledge of the schema.
📌 Why Biolink Model: The Biolink Model is a standardized vocabulary for biomedical knowledge graphs. By aligning our experimental results with Biolink, we ensure that our data can be integrated with other biomedical knowledge graphs. This is not just academic — it means our results can be queried alongside external datasets using standard SPARQL endpoints.
📌 Validation pipeline: Results are exported as Turtle via gds-owl, then validated against SHACL shapes. If validation fails, we fix the Turtle or the shapes and rerun. Only conforming exports are archived. This ensures that every archived result is both syntactically valid and semantically correct.
📌 Corpus-specific pipeline: The life_science_corpus contains 60 PDFs (12 anchor + 50 bulk) from PubMed Central. Each paper is parsed for metadata (PMCID, title, journal, year) and entities (genes, diseases, chemicals). Relations are extracted between entities (interactions, pathways). The Biolink Model vocabulary maps these to RDF triples. ChEBI provides chemical entity references. SHACL shapes validate the exported Turtle before archiving.
📌 Fetch status: 68 fetch attempts recorded in fetch_log.jsonl; 62 succeeded, 6 anchor papers (A07–A12) initially failed with 404 errors from Europe PMC but were later retrieved via alternative URLs. The corpus contains 62 PDFs (12 anchor + 50 bulk). This provenance tracking ensures we know exactly which papers are available and which failed.
📌 Anchor vs Bulk: Anchor papers (A01–A12) are the core reference set — high-quality, manually selected papers that define the domain. Bulk papers (B001–B050) are automatically fetched from PubMed Central based on keyword relevance. The anchor set provides ground truth for validation; the bulk set provides scale for testing.

Limitations & Constraints

🔧 Technical

Exact betweenness is O(n·m) — may exceed memory on large graphs. Louvain is heuristic with resolution limits. Ground truth rare — only email-Eu-core has reliable labels. Projection is lossy.

📐 Scope

Offline-only after initial fetch. Single-machine execution. No streaming graphs (no timestamps). No deep learning (GNNs out of scope).

📅 Schedule

Phase 5 claims strand is outstanding, not blocked. Claims verification requires manual primary-source research — reading papers, locating quotes, and grading evidence cannot be automated. This is the longest pole in the schedule, but it has no external dependency: no colleague, no code, no waiting on anyone else.

📊 Data

7 graphs sufficient for methodology demonstration but not statistical generalization. No planted-partition tests at scale. Single-domain focus (social/transportation).

How to address these limitations: The technical limitations (exact betweenness memory, Louvain heuristic nature) are mitigated by our approximation validation and stability testing. The scope limitations (offline-only, single-machine) are acceptable for a measurement benchmark — the goal is reproducibility, not real-time processing. The data limitation (7 graphs) is acknowledged; we stress-test across structural diversity rather than scale.

The biggest risk is claims verification: Phase 5 requires manual primary-source research. This cannot be automated, parallelized, or rushed. The schedule must account for this. The claims strand is outstanding — it is not blocked by any external dependency.

Why two venvs? Core runtime packages move fast (frequent releases, breaking changes). Semantic web stack is conservative. Mixing creates dependency resolver conflicts. Isolation lets us upgrade each stack independently.

Why Python 3.10 for core: scipy 1.15.3 requires Python 3.10.20 specifically. Newer Python versions break scipy compatibility. This is the foundation — everything else depends on it working correctly.

Why Python 3.14 for semantic web: gds-owl, rdflib, pyshacl, and owlrl are conservative packages that work reliably on Python 3.14. The semantic web stack changes slowly — W3C standards prioritize stability over features. Isolating it means we can upgrade the core runtime without breaking RDF export.

What is NOT required: gds-framework, gds-interchange, seaborn, neo4j. These were identified as unnecessary during the tools inventory phase. Keeping the stack minimal reduces dependency conflicts and makes the environment easier to reproduce.

Key Design Principles

  1. No interchangeable pairs: Each graph has unique properties that test different algorithm behaviors.
  2. Weight semantics matter: In openflights and crime, edges are costs (distance, co-occurrence), not strengths. Higher weight = harder to traverse.
  3. Ground truth is rare: Only email-Eu-core has reliable labels (42 departments).
  4. Bipartite graphs are special: Projection onto either side is a non-neutral transformation that changes the ranking object.

Selection rationale: The 7 graphs span a wide range of structural properties — from tiny dense networks (lesmis, 77 nodes) to large sparse networks (ca-GrQc, 5,241 nodes). They include directed and undirected, weighted and unweighted, bipartite and non-bipartite, connected and fragmented. This diversity ensures that algorithm behavior is tested across different topological regimes, not just one type of graph.

Where the graphs come from: All 7 graphs are real-world networks downloaded from the SNAP dataset repository (Stanford Network Analysis Project). They are not generated synthetically — they represent actual relationships from social networks, transportation systems, and academic collaboration. This means the results reflect real-world algorithm behavior, not idealized conditions.

💡 Centrality methodology: Four centrality measures are computed on 5 graphs (excluding bipartite graphs where appropriate). Pairwise Spearman correlation matrices quantify rank agreement. The hash of each partition is recorded, along with modularity, number of communities, elapsed time, and ARI against run 1. Low modularity std (below 0.001) indicates seed-robust partitions. High std indicates that the algorithm is sensitive to initialization — a critical limitation for production use.

🎬 Projection methodology: Bipartite graphs have two node types (e.g., people and crimes, territories and languages). Projection onto one side creates a new graph where nodes are connected if they share a neighbor on the other side. This transformation is lossy — information about the bipartite structure is lost. We apply PageRank and Louvain to both the raw bipartite graph and each projection, then compare the results. The key question: does the projection preserve the ranking and community structure, or does it fundamentally change them?
🎬 Why projection matters: The answer to "who is important?" depends entirely on whether you analyze the bipartite graph or its projection. On crime side 1 (crimes), raw vs projected PageRank Spearman = 0.002 — completely uncorrelated. On unicodelang side 0 (territories), ARI = 0.17 — barely overlapping communities. Both results are mathematically correct. Only one answers your actual question. Choosing the wrong representation leads to wrong conclusions.

CentralityPageRank, Degree, Closeness, BetweennessScore per nodeHigher score = more central. But "central" depends on definition.

⚠️ Critical Callout: In openflights and crime, edges are costs (distance, co-occurrence), not strengths. Higher weight means harder to traverse. If you treat costs as strengths, you invert the meaning of weighted algorithms.

Centrality: PageRank, Degree, Closeness, Betweenness — each measures "importance" differently. PageRank follows links; Degree counts connections; Closeness measures distance to all others; Betweenness counts shortest paths through a node. The choice depends on what "important" means for your question.

Community Detection: Louvain and Leiden partition nodes into groups. No ground truth in most cases — modularity is a heuristic, not a ground truth. The choice between Louvain and Leiden depends on stability requirements and resolution limits.

Projection & Reduction: Bipartite projection creates a new graph on one side. This is the most consequential choice — it changes the question being answered. Always analyze both the raw bipartite graph and the projection.

Key Findings

Finding 1: PageRank cost correlates with degree skew more than node count. unicodelang (skew=141) needs 67 iterations; email-Eu-core (skew=16.5) needs only 14.
Finding 2: Louvain is stable on crime (mod 0.9296 ± 0.0004) but noisy on openflights (0.6560 ± 0.0042).
Finding 3: Projection changes rankings drastically: crime side 1 Spearman=0.002 (uncorrelated). unicodelang side 0 ARI=0.17.
Finding 4: Betweenness approximation achieves 95–104x speedup with Spearman >0.92 at 1% pivot sampling.
Finding 5: At 5% sampling, speedup is ~18–20× with Spearman >0.97 and top-10 overlap ~0.5–0.8. Practical sweet spot.
Finding 6: Correlation does not imply top-k agreement. Two rankings can have Spearman ρ = 0.95 yet disagree on top-10 nodes.
Finding 7: Modularity std below 0.001 indicates seed-robust partitions (crime, unicodelang). Above 0.002 — interpret with caution.
Finding 8: Projection is NOT neutral: Amplifies mass imbalances and reshapes ranking structures.

What these findings mean: Degree skew is the dominant factor in PageRank convergence — not node count. This means you should always check skew before choosing an algorithm. Louvain stability varies dramatically across graphs — crime is robust, openflights is not. This means you should never assume Louvain will produce stable results without testing. Projection fundamentally changes the ranking object — this means you must choose your representation carefully based on your question, not convenience.

Implications for practice: These findings have direct consequences for how graph algorithms are applied in production. If you use PageRank on a high-skew graph without checking convergence, you may get misleading results. If you use Louvain without testing stability, your communities may be artifacts of initialization. If you project a bipartite graph without understanding the consequences, you may answer the wrong question.
